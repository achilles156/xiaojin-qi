Research

My research lies at the intersection of urban history, archaeology, architectural history, and heritage studies, with a particular focus on historical cities and landscapes in China.

I investigate how cities and built environments took shape through the interaction of environmental conditions, material practices, social institutions, and systems of knowledge. Rather than treating urban form as a fixed physical object, I am interested in how historical places were constructed, inhabited, connected to wider networks, and transformed over time.

My approach brings historical texts into dialogue with archaeological remains, buildings, topography, waterways, and evidence gathered through fieldwork. I am particularly interested in what these different kinds of evidence can reveal when examined together, as well as the methodological challenges involved in connecting material observations with historical interpretations.

Historical Cities and Landscapes

My research on historical cities examines the relationships between urban form, environmental settings, social networks, and everyday life.

One central concern is the relationship between mobility and residentiality in the formation and transformation of historical cities. Mobility encompasses the circulation of people, goods, capital, and knowledge, while residentiality concerns the conditions and practices that sustain urban life in particular places. My work on Qixian, a historical commercial city in Shanxi, explores how the decline of long-distance commercial networks transformed local economic and social structures, and how residential practices continued to sustain the city after those networks weakened.

Related research on cities such as Quanzhou, Huizhou, and Rizhao considers how topography, waterways, regional connections, and historically accumulated cultural meanings shaped urban development.

These investigations contribute to a broader interest in understanding historical urban landscapes as evolving relationships between the built environment, natural settings, and social practices.

Material Knowledge and Spatial Practices

A second area of research concerns the material and technical practices through which historical environments were produced, organized, and understood.

My work on ceramic production in Jingdezhen examines kiln technologies, production organization, and the transmission of craft knowledge. It considers how archaeological evidence can contribute to reconstructing technological practices and broader transformations in systems of production.

I am also interested in historical practices of surveying, orientation, and construction, particularly the relationships between practical techniques, institutional arrangements, and cosmological ideas. Rather than assuming a direct correspondence between spatial form and intellectual traditions, I seek to identify the material procedures and historical circumstances through which such relationships may have been established.

A related line of inquiry explores religious institutions and networks of knowledge in northern China, especially during the Liao, Jin, and Yuan periods.

Across these subjects, I approach technical and spatial practices not merely as applications of abstract knowledge, but as important sites in which knowledge was produced, transmitted, and transformed.

Archaeology and Heritage Interpretation

My third research interest concerns the relationship between material evidence, historical interpretation, and contemporary heritage practices.

Archaeological remains, architectural structures, historical landscapes, and written records preserve different and often incomplete traces of the past. Understanding their relationships requires attention to the conditions under which evidence was produced, preserved, documented, and interpreted.

I am interested in developing methods that integrate archaeological reasoning, historical research, and the close observation of built and natural environments. Particular methodological concerns include the reconstruction of historical processes, the evaluation of competing explanations, and the distinction between material observations and interpretive hypotheses.

These questions also inform my work on cultural heritage. Rather than understanding heritage primarily as a collection of protected objects, I examine how historical places continue to acquire meaning through changing patterns of use, collective memory, and contemporary interpretation.

My broader aim is to explore how a materially grounded approach to historical interpretation can contribute both to our understanding of past societies and to the ways historical environments are studied, conserved, and experienced today.
